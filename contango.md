### What Is Contango?

Contango is a market condition where the price of a futures or forward contract is higher than the current spot price of the underlying asset, 
creating an upward sloping forward curve. This is common in commodities, interest rate products, volatility products, and credit markets.

For example:

Spot oil price today: $70/barrel
Oil futures, 6 months out: $75/barrel

The market is in contango because the future delivery price exceeds the current price.

The opposite condition is backwardation, where futures prices are below the spot price.

Why Does Contango Exist?

The most common reason is the cost of carry.

Owning a commodity today may involve:

Storage costs
Insurance costs
Financing costs
Opportunity cost of capital

These costs get incorporated into futures prices.

Consider crude oil:

Oil must be stored in tanks
Storage costs money
Capital is tied up

As a result, buying oil for future delivery can be more expensive than buying it today.

Why Traders Care About Contango

Many traders incorrectly assume that if the market price does not move, they will neither make nor lose money.

In contango markets this is often false.

As futures contracts approach expiry, they tend to converge toward the spot price. If spot prices remain unchanged, a futures contract in contango generally loses value over time.

This creates what traders call:

Roll decay
Negative roll yield
Carry loss
Example 1: Oil Futures

Suppose:

Contract	PriceSpot Oil	$70
3 Month Future	$72
6 Month Future	$75

Oil is in contango.

You buy the 6 month futures contract at $75.

Six months later:

Oil spot price remains $70.
Futures must converge to spot.
Contract expires near $70.

Result:

Bought at $75
Sold at $70
Lost $5

Even though the actual oil price never moved.

This surprises many new futures traders.

Contango and Commodity ETFs

A famous example is oil ETFs.

Many commodity ETFs do not own physical oil. They own futures contracts and must continually roll them.

Suppose:

Sell expiring future at $70
Buy next month future at $75

Every roll loses $5.

Repeated over months, this can seriously damage returns even if spot oil barely changes. This effect became widely discussed in crude oil ETFs during periods of persistent contango.

How Contango Affects Options

Options on futures inherit many effects from the futures curve.

Example: Call Option on Crude Oil Futures

Suppose:

Spot oil = $70
6 month future = $75
You buy a call option on the futures contract.

Many inexperienced traders look only at spot oil and expect profits if oil stays flat.

However:

Futures contract drifts toward spot
Underlying futures loses value
Call option can lose value despite stable spot prices

The option experiences a hidden headwind from the futures roll down.

Calendar Spread Example

A trader buys:

Long 6 month call
Short 1 month call

Contango means:

Longer dated futures are richer
Longer dated options often embed that richer futures level

The economics of the spread are affected by the shape of the futures curve, not just volatility.

VIX Products and Contango

One of the most famous contango trades involves volatility.

The VIX futures curve is frequently in contango.

Example:

Contract	VIX LevelSpot VIX	15
1 Month Future	17
2 Month Future	19

An investor buying VIX futures ETF products:

Sells lower priced expiring contracts
Buys higher priced later contracts

Month after month.

This creates systematic losses from negative roll yield.

Historically many long volatility ETFs suffered from this effect during calm markets.

Contango in Credit Default Swaps (CDS)

Contango also appears in credit markets, although traders may not always use the term as frequently as they do in commodities.

A CDS contract is insurance against default.

Consider a company whose default risk is perceived to be increasing.

CDS spreads might look like:

Maturity	CDS Spread1 Year	100 bps
3 Year	150 bps
5 Year	200 bps

This upward sloping term structure resembles contango.

The market is pricing more credit risk in later years.

Impact on CDS Traders

Suppose a trader buys a 5 year CDS.

If the company's credit quality remains unchanged:

The 5 year contract ages
It becomes a 4 year contract
Eventually a 3 year contract

The spread may naturally decline along the curve.

This can create carry effects similar to futures roll down.

Credit traders therefore focus heavily on:

Carry
Roll
Curve positioning

Not just default risk itself.

A trader can be correct about the company's survival and still lose money because of adverse movement along the CDS curve.

Interest Rate Markets

Contango-like structures exist throughout fixed income markets.

For example:

Instrument	Yield2 Year Treasury	3.5%
10 Year Treasury	4.2%

Longer maturity instruments trade at different levels from short maturity instruments.

Traders often structure:

Curve steepener trades
Curve flattener trades
Forward rate agreements
Interest rate swaps

Profit depends not only on direction but also on how the term structure evolves.

Trading Opportunities Created by Contango
1. Cash and Carry Arbitrage

Suppose:

Spot gold = $2,000
Futures = $2,100

A trader may:

Buy gold today
Store it
Sell futures

If the price difference exceeds storage and financing costs, an arbitrage profit may exist.

2. Short Futures Carry Trades

Some professional traders prefer shorting futures in persistent contango markets because convergence often works in their favor.

3. Relative Value Trading

Traders compare:

Near contracts
Far contracts

Seeking opportunities where the curve may flatten or steepen.

The Key Lesson

Most new traders focus only on the direction of the underlying asset.

Professional traders also focus on the shape of the curve.

Contango means:

Future prices are above spot prices.
Contracts tend to lose value as they converge toward spot.
Long futures positions face negative roll yield.
Commodity ETFs can underperform because of rolling costs.
Options on futures inherit the economics of the futures curve.
CDS, swap, volatility, and interest rate traders all monitor term structure effects because carry and roll can materially affect profits.

A useful rule of thumb is:

In a strongly contangoed market, simply being long is often not enough. The asset must rise by more than the carry and roll costs just to break even.
