## Edward de Bono's Lateral Thinking

Edward de Bono introduced Lateral Thinking in 1967 as a deliberate method for solving problems by moving away from established patterns of thought. Traditional thinking tends to be analytical and sequential. It examines existing assumptions, compares alternatives, and chooses the best option from those already visible.

Lateral Thinking takes a different approach. Instead of asking:

"Which of these options is best?"

it asks:

"What options have we not considered?"

The goal is not to improve existing ideas incrementally. The goal is to generate entirely new ideas by challenging assumptions, disrupting established patterns, and looking at problems from unexpected directions.

De Bono's argument was based on how the brain forms patterns. Once people develop expertise, they become more efficient at recognizing familiar situations. This efficiency is useful, but it can also create blind spots. People repeatedly follow the same mental pathways and become less likely to notice alternatives.

Lateral Thinking is an attempt to break those pathways.

Vertical Thinking Versus Lateral Thinking

De Bono often contrasted lateral thinking with what he called vertical thinking.

Vertical Thinking	Lateral ThinkingSequential	Non sequential
Focuses on correctness	Focuses on possibilities
Eliminates bad ideas early	Delays judgment
Looks for the best answer	Looks for additional answers
Uses logic to follow a path	Uses provocation to create new paths
Improves existing solutions	Creates alternative solutions

For example, imagine traffic congestion in a city.

Vertical thinking:

Add more roads
Improve traffic lights
Expand junctions
Increase parking capacity

These are logical extensions of existing assumptions.

Lateral thinking:

What if employees received financial incentives not to travel at peak times?
What if parking became mobile rather than fixed?
What if offices operated in rotating schedules?
What if delivery vehicles only operated overnight?

Rather than optimizing the current system, it challenges the structure itself.

### The Core Principles of Lateral Thinking
1. Challenge Assumptions

Most decisions are built on beliefs that nobody questions.

For example:

"Customers must come to our store."

Amazon challenged that assumption.

"What if the store came to the customer?"

The result was a fundamentally different business model.

Many breakthroughs begin by examining assumptions that people treat as facts.

Questions might include:

Why do we do it this way?
What if this rule disappeared?
Who says this constraint must exist?
What if the opposite were true?
2. Escape Established Patterns

Human beings are naturally pattern seeking.

This makes routine decisions efficient but can limit creativity.

For example, hotel companies traditionally competed through:

Better rooms
Better locations
Better service

Airbnb escaped the pattern entirely.

Instead of asking:

"How do we build a better hotel?"

they asked:

"Do we need to own hotels at all?"

This created an entirely different market.

3. Use Provocation

One of de Bono's most famous techniques was provocation.

The idea is to deliberately create a statement that appears wrong, absurd, or impossible.

For example:

"Cars should have square wheels."

Obviously this is impractical.

However, the purpose is not to implement the idea.

It is to ask:

Why are wheels round?
What benefits does a round wheel provide?
Can technology provide those benefits differently?

Provocative thinking often uncovers hidden assumptions.

De Bono referred to these provocative statements as PO statements.

4. Random Entry

Random stimuli can disrupt predictable thinking.

Suppose a company is trying to improve customer support.

A random word generator produces:

"Garden"

The team might ask:

How do gardens grow?
How are gardens maintained?
How do ecosystems self regulate?

This could inspire ideas such as:

Self service support communities
Customer mentoring programs
Layered support ecosystems

The random word is not the answer. It is merely a trigger.

Key Lateral Thinking Techniques
Concept Extraction

Extract the underlying concept from a successful idea and apply it elsewhere.

Example:

Uber's concept is not ride sharing.

Its deeper concept is:

Matching unused capacity with real time demand.

The same concept applies to:

Accommodation (Airbnb)
Freight logistics
Cloud computing resources
Equipment rental platforms
Reversal

Take an assumption and reverse it.

Example:

Restaurant assumption:

Customers come to restaurants.

Reversal:

Restaurants go to customers.

This idea contributes to:

Food delivery services
Pop up restaurants
Mobile food trucks
Fractionation

Break a system into components and redesign each separately.

Example:

Universities traditionally bundled:

Teaching
Accreditation
Campus facilities
Career networking

Online learning platforms such as Coursera and Udemy effectively separated these components.

### Analogies

Apply insights from unrelated industries.

For example:

Hospitals adopted techniques from Formula One pit stops when redesigning operating room handoffs and critical care processes.

Looking outside the immediate industry can reveal solutions that insiders overlook.

How Lateral Thinking Is Used in Business
Product Innovation

Perhaps the most common use.

Companies use lateral thinking to identify:

New products
New customer segments
New pricing models
New business models

Examples include:

Netflix

Traditional thinking:

Improve video rentals.

Lateral thinking:

Eliminate rentals entirely.

Then later:

Eliminate physical media entirely.

Apple

When developing the iPhone, Apple was not merely designing a better telephone.

It combined:

Computer
Camera
Music player
Internet device

into a new category.

Southwest Airlines

Rather than competing with traditional airlines directly, Southwest asked:

What if air travel operated more like bus transportation?

This led to simplified operations and lower costs.

Strategic Planning

Executives often become trapped by industry assumptions.

Lateral thinking can help examine:

Alternative markets
Alternative revenue models
Alternative partnerships
Emerging technologies

When industries face disruption, conventional analysis alone is often insufficient because the future may not resemble the past.

Process Improvement

Organizations frequently optimize inefficient processes rather than questioning them.

Example:

Instead of asking:

How do we speed up this approval process?

Lateral thinkers may ask:

Why does the approval process exist?

Eliminating a process can create greater gains than automating it.

### Problem Solving

Particularly useful when:

A problem has persisted for years.
Conventional solutions have failed.
Multiple stakeholders are deadlocked.
Existing assumptions are creating barriers.
Application to Business Decisions

Lateral Thinking should not replace financial analysis, market research, or operational planning.

Instead, it should be used earlier in the decision process.

A practical business decision framework looks like this:

Stage 1: Generate Possibilities

Use lateral thinking.

Goal:

Maximize options.
Challenge assumptions.
Create novel alternatives.
Stage 2: Evaluate Options

Use conventional analysis.

Goal:

Cost analysis
Risk assessment
Feasibility studies
Financial modelling
Stage 3: Execute

Use project management and operational controls.

Many organizations fail because they jump directly to Stage 2 without first expanding the range of possible solutions.

### Why Organizations Use Lateral Thinking
Innovation

Many competitive advantages come from finding opportunities others overlook.

Incremental improvements are easier for competitors to copy.

Novel business models are often harder to imitate.

Breaking Organizational Inertia

Large organizations develop habits and procedures that become accepted wisdom.

Lateral thinking helps challenge:

Legacy processes
Organisational assumptions
Historical decisions

This is particularly important in mature industries.

Responding to Disruption

Industries can change rapidly.

Examples include:

Streaming replacing DVDs
Cloud computing replacing on premises infrastructure
Digital photography replacing film

Companies that rely solely on past experience may struggle to adapt.

Competitive Differentiation

Competing solely on price often becomes a race to the bottom.

Lateral thinking can reveal entirely different ways to create value.

### Strengths of Lateral Thinking
Encourages Innovation

Its greatest strength is generating genuinely new ideas.

Many breakthrough products and business models emerge from questioning basic assumptions.

Helps Solve Difficult Problems

When traditional approaches repeatedly fail, a new perspective may uncover overlooked solutions.

Reveals Hidden Assumptions

Organizations often discover that many "constraints" are self imposed rather than real.

Creates Strategic Flexibility

Businesses become less dependent on existing models and more open to change.

Improves Adaptability

Teams learn to respond more effectively to uncertainty and disruption.

### Weaknesses of Lateral Thinking
Ideas Can Be Impractical

Not every creative idea survives contact with reality.

Many generated concepts fail when tested against economics, regulations, or technical constraints.

Can Be Time Consuming

Exploring unconventional possibilities requires time and resources.

Organizations under immediate operational pressure may struggle to apply it consistently.

Risk of Group Frustration

Analytical thinkers sometimes become uncomfortable when ideas are deliberately illogical or provocative.

This can create resistance.

Difficult to Measure

Traditional business processes often involve clear metrics.

The value of creative exploration is harder to quantify.

Not Suitable for Every Problem

If a problem is well understood and has an established solution, lateral thinking can add unnecessary complexity.

For example:

Regulatory compliance
Payroll processing
Safety procedures
Tax reporting

These areas usually benefit more from precision and consistency than creativity.

### Criticisms of Lateral Thinking

Some critics argue that de Bono overstated the distinction between lateral and traditional creativity.

Others suggest that many of his techniques simply formalize brainstorming practices that already existed.

A common criticism is that Lateral Thinking excels at generating ideas but provides little guidance on selecting and implementing them.

This criticism is largely valid.

Lateral Thinking is not a complete decision making system. It is an idea generation system.

Organizations still need:

Strategic analysis
Financial modelling
Risk management
Operational planning

to determine whether generated ideas should be implemented.

### The Real Value of Lateral Thinking

The real contribution of Edward de Bono's work is not that it guarantees better ideas. It is that it provides a structured way of escaping habitual thinking.

Most organizations spend the majority of their time evaluating options that already exist. Lateral Thinking focuses on creating options that do not yet exist.

That distinction is important.

A team performing a discounted cash flow analysis, assessing risks, or comparing investment opportunities is engaging in vertical thinking. A team asking whether the entire business model, pricing structure, customer relationship, or market definition should be reconsidered is engaging in lateral thinking.

The most effective organizations use both.

Lateral Thinking generates possibilities.

Critical thinking determines which of those possibilities are worth pursuing.

Neither is sufficient on its own. Used together, they provide a balance between creativity and disciplined decision making, which is where many of the most significant business innovations have originated.
